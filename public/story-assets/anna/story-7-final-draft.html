Anna Bogle and the Gap in the Gold

An Irish story of rain, bargains, old promises, and the gift that was not for sale.

Ryan's tooth had wobbled through breakfast, through spelling, and through one very serious argument with an apple.

By supper, it was hanging on from pure stubbornness.

Then Ryan bit his toast and the tooth gave up.

It landed in his palm, tiny and white and suddenly much more important than toast.

Ryan grinned through the new gap.

"So," he said, "how much gold is this worth?"

Grandad nearly choked on his tea.

"Who told you gold was the point?" Grandad asked.

"Everyone," said Ryan.

"Everyone is a noisy person," said Grandad. "Often wrong."

Grandad leaned back in his chair.

"When I was small, I heard an older version. Stranger, too. There was a keeper named Anna Bogle."

"She came for a child's tooth and left gold behind, but only if the gold was..."

Grandad stopped.

The middle of the story seemed to go thin.

"Expensive?" Ryan guessed.

"No," said Grandad. "Older than expensive."

He tapped his cup, trying to find the lost word.

"It was for something."

Ryan placed his tooth in a folded bit of paper beside his pillow.

Under it, he tucked a drawing of himself, Grandad, and a coin as big as a biscuit.

Far away and not far at all, Tanda felt a cold fleck appear on her map.

Ireland glimmered, then dimmed at one small cottage.

In a place under a field wall, Anna Bogle's gold pouch gave a sour little clink.

Anna narrowed her eyes.

"Oh no. Not that shine."

Ryan tried to stay awake.

He lasted seven minutes, which he considered heroic.

Then the window clicked.

Then the curtain sneezed rain.

Anna Bogle burst through the gap in the curtains, wrestling an umbrella twice her size.

She landed in a sock drawer and said, "I meant to do that."

She shook rain from her coat, spotted Ryan's open eye, and sighed.

"Excellent. A witness. I do my best work with supervision."

Ryan sat up.

"Are you Anna Bogle?"

"Depends who is asking."

"Ryan."

"Then yes. If a tax collector asks, I am a hat."

Anna lifted the tooth.

Her face softened for one breath.

Then she saw the huge coin in Ryan's drawing, and her mouth snapped shut again.

"Ah," she said. "The tooth has brought friends. Expectation. Arithmetic. Trouble in pajamas."

Ryan looked down at his pajamas.

"They are good pajamas."

The gold in Anna's pouch gave another cold clink.

Beside it, a thread of shadow curled where no shadow should fit.

Anna's eyes went flat.

A soft light touched the sill.

Tanda hovered there, wings folded small against the rain.

"Anna," she said, "I came to ask."

"I know what you came with," Anna said.

"A grand idea. A shining net. A place where every small thing can become one more thing to count."

Tanda lowered her hands.

"Then I should listen first."

Anna blinked.

She had prepared several insults and now had nowhere tidy to put them.

Grandad woke in the chair by the door.

He stared at Anna, then at the tooth, then at Tanda.

"Well," he said softly, "my mother was not making you up."

The room hushed.

Grandad tried again.

"The gold was not for buying the tooth. It was for..."

The missing part of the old story thinned like paper in rain.

The shadow near the pouch lifted its head.

Anna snapped the gold shut.

"Do not feed that," she said.

Ryan whispered, "Feed what?"

"A bad habit with a long body," Anna said.

"And I will not name it in a child's bedroom."

Anna turned to Tanda.

"You say your Tooth Fee is not a price. Show me the difference."

Tanda looked at Ryan's tooth, and memory opened.

Young Tanda stood in a shipyard with one tooth missing and one bright coin beside her hand.

"If I take the coin," she asked, "is it still mine?"

Her father knelt so his eyes were level with hers.

"Yes. A fee is not a purchase. It is a promise to notice."

The next morning he threaded her tooth onto a leather cord.

"May I carry what is yours?" he asked.

Young Tanda held the cord until the knot was done.

Later, across cold water, that tooth tapped near his heart when anger would have been easy.

It reminded him whose promise he carried.

The cottage returned.

Tanda's wings glowed low and warm.

"My father did not buy my tooth," she said. "He carried my growing with care."

Anna studied her.

"You remembered him before you defended yourself."

Tanda nodded.

"I am trying to build something that helps people remember. But I still have to be taught."

Ryan frowned at the pouch.

"So gold is bad?"

Anna looked horrified.

"Gold? Bad? Wash your mouth with rainwater. Gold is grand."

She held up one finger.

"Gold is a fine servant and a dreadful boss."

She held up a second.

"And if you let it boss a tooth, the tooth gets lonely."

"Then what should the gold prove?" Ryan asked.

"That you lost a tooth?"

Anna made a face.

"Half a point. A damp half."

Grandad's eyes brightened.

"That someone noticed you were growing."

The missing part of the old story clicked back into place.

The coin in Anna's pouch warmed.

Not brighter than the tooth.

Not bigger than the story.

Just warm enough to say: yes, that.

Ryan took his drawing and turned it over.

On the back he wrote, very carefully, "Grandad remembered Anna Bogle when my tooth fell out."

Anna wrapped the tooth in a clover cloth.

"This one is not for sale," she said.

"This one is for keeping."

She placed a small gold piece beside the drawing, not on top of it.

"There," she said. "Proof that somebody noticed."

A few gold flecks appeared at the corner of Ryan's drawing.

A clover glow curled around the edge.

One glimmer settled beside Grandad's name.

The shadow by the pouch pulled back into a stone crack, thinner than a thread.

Anna saw it go.

So did Tanda.

Neither of them smiled.

At the window, Anna spoke low.

"When old promises go quiet, something comes to eat the quiet. I will help you watch. I will not be managed."

Tanda bowed her head.

"Then help me ask better."

Anna's gap-toothed smile finally appeared.

"A terrible name, Network. We will discuss it."

The rain thinned.

Beyond the cottage, small threads of Toothlight reached toward other keepers, other windows, other promises.

Anna did not let go of her pouch.

Tanda did not ask her to.

They stood watch together.

Morning found Ryan with a gold piece, a glimmering drawing, and a story Grandad could remember whole.

The gold was lovely.

The remembering was better.

Inspired by Irish leprechaun folklore, old gift-for-tooth traditions, and the Tooth Fairy Network's fictional keeper Anna Bogle. Ryan's story is framed as one family's older version, remembered by Grandad.
